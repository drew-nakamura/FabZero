# Deliver useful hardware before selling RTL

**The RTL distinction matters, but the customer must have a reason to need a digital hardware component.** A business that merely wants to recognize a red label already has credible microcontroller and software alternatives. The earlier package-checker recommendation established a visible interaction and manageable algorithm; it did not establish why its customer should choose FPGA hardware. The strongest initial customer hypothesis is a small technical hardware team that needs one defined processing, interface or control block and is blocked on implementing and integrating it. Its useful purchase would be a tested, reusable prototype on a supported board, with enough evidence to continue development. This is a recommendation for discovery, not validated demand or an accepted product scope. No customer interview, payment, comparative performance test or board execution has been established by this research.

Prepared November 14, 2026, America/Los_Angeles. The research concerns the product discussed as FabZero; the latest local decision record names it **SiliconeOS**. The requested filename and existing repository paths retain the historical name. That naming decision does not change the customer or scope. Three read-only research passes examined technical alternatives, named engineering cases and competing tools; the evidence below is primarily official documentation and supplier-published customer material.

## The same visible result can come from different architectures

Arduino's official Nicla Vision tutorial demonstrates color and brightness blob detection in MicroPython, image annotations and red/green indications. It explicitly includes identifying missing assembly pieces as an application. That is close enough to the proposed red-label checker to make the concern concrete: **the pass/reject interaction alone supplies little evidence of FPGA necessity**. An Arduino-style implementation can be an appropriate professional choice. ([Arduino blob-detection tutorial](https://docs.arduino.cc/tutorials/nicla-vision/blob-detection/))

RTL, or register-transfer level, describes digital hardware: how stored values change and how signals are processed between clock events. Synthesis maps that description into a circuit in the FPGA. Microcontroller software supplies instructions for an existing processor to execute. Both can compute the same answer, but their interfaces, concurrency and timing behavior can differ. RTL simulation checks the described behavior; physical implementation and timing analysis establish additional evidence. Device-specific libraries and board integration also limit portability. ([AMD RTL simulation guidance](https://docs.amd.com/r/2025.2-English/ug900-vivado-logic-simulation/Behavioral-Simulation-at-the-Register-Transfer-Level))

The relevant distinction is therefore the required hardware contract. A customer can need continuous stream processing, a bounded response between defined interfaces, multiple concurrent channels, precise signal generation or a reusable block for a larger design. Streaming hardware can accept new inputs while earlier inputs remain in processing, so sustained acceptance rate and total latency require separate checks. These properties must be specified and measured. An internal fixed-cycle result does not establish fixed camera-to-actuator delay when capture, operating-system scheduling or buffering sits outside the tested boundary. ([AMD streaming-interface guidance](https://docs.amd.com/r/2024.2-English/ug1399-vitis-hls/Modeling-Streaming-Data-Interfaces))

None of those requirements automatically excludes a processor. MCU timers, DMA, specialized peripherals, CPUs, GPUs and existing smart cameras can solve different subsets. Even FPGA vendor Microchip discusses cost and power cases favoring processors. The team should compare the actual candidate architectures against the real constraints, rather than add difficult requirements solely to justify an FPGA. A small circuit can be valuable; a complicated circuit can be unnecessary. ([Microchip architectural tradeoffs](https://developerhelp.microchip.com/xwiki/bin/view/products/fpga/hello-fpga/why-use/))

## Existing customers buy specific timing and integration outcomes

A named NI case study from Sagemcom describes an industrial test cell requiring synchronized parallel operations and a triggered measurement with a 10 microsecond window. The authors explain how their FPGA-based arrangement addressed that timing problem. This is stronger customer context than a generic desire for automation because it identifies a concrete constraint. The publication is customer/vendor evidence, and its wider production improvements should not all be attributed to the FPGA. ([Sagemcom production-test case](https://www.ni.com/en/solutions/energy/case-studies/automation-of-a-robotized-production-cell-through-compactrio.html))

Infineon describes reprogrammable FPGA applications for testing microcontroller peripherals, including digital protocols and configurable pulse behavior. Its use of LabVIEW FPGA also shows that customers seek the capability rather than necessarily wanting to write Verilog themselves. These cases support instrumentation, sensor-test and custom-interface work as relevant categories. They do not prove that those organizations would purchase this product or that their systems fit the KV260. ([Infineon test-system case](https://www.ni.com/en/solutions/semiconductor/case-studies/creating-an-automated-microcontroller-test-system-using-labview-and-fpga.html))

Imaging offers both supporting evidence and counterevidence. Basler places FPGA preprocessing near acquisition for filtering and feature extraction while retaining processor and GPU roles. An AMD/Axiomtek case uses the same K26 SOM family for responsive embedded vision. Yet AMD also publishes an Advantech optical-inspection architecture using CPU/GPU computation. **Camera inspection is a real application category, but it does not select the architecture for us.** These are supplier descriptions, not reproduced comparative benchmarks for our board. ([Basler preprocessing](https://www.baslerweb.com/en-us/capabilities/fpga-based-vision-solution/), [Axiomtek K26 case](https://www.amd.com/content/dam/amd/en/documents/resources/case-studies/axiomtek-case-study.pdf), [Advantech inspection case](https://www.amd.com/content/dam/amd/en/documents/resources/case-studies/aoi-advantech-case-study.pdf))

My resulting customer hypothesis is a small company developing an instrument, camera module, sensor-processing system or specialized controller that already has a reason to evaluate custom digital hardware. The operator is an embedded/electronics engineer or technical founder who can define behavior and approve results but lacks available FPGA specialist time. The buyer is the CTO, hardware lead or founder responsible for the prototype milestone. The trigger is one component blocking a design decision, integration milestone or customer demonstration. The proposed reason to pay is less engineering effort and uncertainty in reaching that milestone. Each element remains to be validated.

A company with only a broad invention idea often needs architecture and requirements work first. Lack of RTL-writing experience is compatible with being a useful customer; lack of a defined job creates a different engagement. The product can help clarify a contract, but somebody must confirm inputs, outputs, error cases and observable success. “No RTL expertise required” should not imply that no engineering decisions remain.

## Generation and verification already have serious alternatives

Cadence's February 2026 ChipStack announcement markets design and testbench generation, test planning, regressions and debug using EDA tools. ChipAgents likewise advertises RTL and verification workflows and publishes named model/bus-interface verification cases. Their productivity figures and capability descriptions are supplier claims, but the competitive implication is clear: **generating RTL, and even checking it, are already marketed capabilities**. There is no evidence here that these tools are interchangeable or cannot support physical deployment. ([Cadence ChipStack announcement](https://www.cadence.com/en_US/home/company/newsroom/press-releases/pr/2026/cadence-unleashes-chipstack-ai-super-agent-pioneering-a-new.html), [ChipAgents capabilities](https://chipagents.ai/ic-star), [ChipAgents cases](https://chipagents.ai/customers))

Other routes also matter. AMD high-level synthesis generates RTL from C/C++ algorithms, with simulation and architectural exploration, while still requiring interface and performance decisions. Existing IP can remove the need to generate a component at all. General coding agents already operate repositories and tools. Engineering services compete on the complete outcome: Enclustra describes specification, FPGA firmware, embedded software, verification and prototypes, with named sensor-test and measurement customers. ([AMD HLS benefits](https://docs.amd.com/r/2025.2-English/ug1399-vitis-hls/Benefits-of-High-Level-Synthesis), [Claude Code overview](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/overview), [Enclustra development services](https://www.enclustra.com/en/design-services/))

A proposed positioning is: **SiliconeOS helps small hardware teams turn a defined digital component into a tested, reusable FPGA prototype on a supported board.** Its delivery package would contain the behavior/interface contract, reusable source, independent tests, constraints, implementation evidence and repeatable build/load/run instructions. A physical demonstration would show actual component results. This is a narrow promise worth testing, not a discovered market gap or defensible moat. The research has established neither acceptable pricing nor a reason a particular buyer would switch from their existing engineer, contractor, IP or toolchain.

Some customers intend to ship FPGA-based products; others use FPGAs to exercise digital blocks before an ASIC is fabricated. Synopsys describes the latter as executing synthesizable RTL for hardware/software verification. These are different jobs. A KV260 prototype can support digital behavior and integration claims, but it does not establish a future chip's area, power, analog behavior, manufacturing readiness or achievable ASIC clock. Reusable core RTL and a board-specific deployment package should be identified separately. ([Synopsys FPGA-prototyping explanation](https://www.synopsys.com/blogs/chip-design/scalable-performance-for-soc-design.html))

## A stronger camera demo needs a hardware component contract

The camera can still make the business story understandable. A better hypothetical request comes from a camera-module or robotics-electronics team: “Prototype a stage that consumes our pixel stream, examines selected image regions, emits a result through our agreed interface, and meets our response and input-rate requirements.” Judges could see a live image and a visibly checked result, while the source, interface, tests and measured implementation establish what was delivered. The FPGA should do the claimed pixel work. If software already supplies an object-detected Boolean, the hardware evidence concerns a control block rather than image recognition.

An occasional crop transferred through registers is an honest functional prototype. It does not demonstrate continuous camera-rate processing. A streaming claim needs actual integrated stream acceptance, output correctness, latency and loss/backpressure evidence. No frame rate, speedup or processor inadequacy should be invented to make that claim attractive. The business proposition can concern delivery of a needed prototype without proving the cheapest final production architecture, but the need for the hardware artifact must be plausible.

There is also a less visual but well-grounded alternative: an instrumentation customer requests a multichannel event timestamp and trigger block. A live timeline could show event order and specified response cycles. Such a function becomes valuable when the actual timing/channel/interface requirements justify custom hardware. A labeled on-chip event generator can support reproducible initial tests, but it does not prove external acquisition or physical sensor operation. Existing MCU peripherals must remain an honest alternative.

The current KV260 has no verified working baseline. Earlier read-only planning found evidence suggesting missing K26 device support in the local Vivado installation, and camera capture/custom-core coexistence is unproven. AMD documents an AR1335 reference capture path, but a stock camera application is acquisition scaffolding. Loading a different bitstream can remove that path; camera capture and the target component must work in the same supported design/runtime. Installed Vivado/Vitis 2025.2 does not establish compatibility with an older camera reference platform. No camera, simulation, build or board-stage success was produced by this research. ([AMD camera platforms](https://docs.amd.com/r/en-US/ug1089-kv260-starter-kit/Vitis-Platforms), [AR1335 reference setup](https://xilinx.github.io/kria-apps-docs/kv260/2022.1/build/html/docs/smartcamera/docs/app_deployment.html))

## Validate the blocked component before choosing the spectacle

The next useful customer conversation should begin with the last component the person tried to prototype, its board/interfaces, and what prevented completion. Ask why software, MCU peripherals, existing IP or HLS did or did not suffice; identify where time went and which evidence would make the result trustworthy. Then establish who approves spending and what alternative they would choose. An observed real example with their acceptance criteria is stronger than asking whether an attractive idea sounds useful. No outreach is authorized or performed by this report.

The teammate retains ownership of the main product specification. This research proposes qualifying customers by a real component need and choosing the demonstration afterward. A clearly visible camera application remains useful presentation, and a modest function can remain the implementation scope. The correction is to connect that function to the customer's hardware requirement before accepting it as the product demonstration. The existing judging reference also leaves customer/problem and acquisition evidence unresolved; extra camera complexity cannot supply those outcomes.
